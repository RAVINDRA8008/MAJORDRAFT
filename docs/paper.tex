\begin{abstract}
EEG-based emotion recognition suffers from data scarcity and high inter-subject variability, limiting the generalization of deep learning models. This study proposes an adaptive multimodal framework that combines a conditional generative adversarial network (GAN) for synthesizing class-specific EEG features with a Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) agent that dynamically controls the augmentation ratio based on real-time validation feedback. The system further enhances robustness through late fusion of EEG features from the DEAP dataset with speech emotion embeddings from the IEMOCAP database. Experimental results demonstrate that the proposed approach achieves 88.4\% accuracy in subject-dependent and 72.1\% in subject-independent evaluation on four-class emotion classification, outperforming static augmentation baselines by up to 6.2\%.

\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
EEG emotion recognition, generative adversarial networks, deep reinforcement learning, multimodal affective computing, speech emotion recognition.
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}

Physiological emotion recognition is one of the most important issues in affective computing, with a number of applications in the design of immersive and adaptive systems. Among the available modalities, EEG provides high-resolution insight into emotion-related brain activity but remains limited by data scarcity and strong inter-subject variability \cite{koelstra2012deap,abhang2016book}.

Currently available EEG-based emotion recognition systems have poor generalization capabilities because of various interlinked problems. High acquisition costs of EEG data result in the generation of small datasets, which impacts EEG-based deep models, making them overfitted. Variability of electrode locations, brain structures, and cognitive patterns also results in domain shift problems, which are poorly handled by current data augmentation methods \cite{koelstra2012deap,li2020autoencoder}.

Several GAN-based approaches, such as PSD-GAN \cite{pan2021gan} and VAE-D2GAN \cite{bao2021gan}, have been proposed to augment EEG data \cite{habashi2023gan}. However, most of the existing literature uses fixed augmentation ratios and disregards the model's convergence dynamics, which might result in insufficient augmentation during the initial stages and an overload of synthesized data during later stages, causing deviations during training. Additionally, GANs face the issue of mode collapse and lack mechanisms for evaluating the quality of synthesized data \cite{luo2020augmentation}.

Reinforcement learning can be used to adaptively control the process of data augmentation by modeling it as a decision-making process that is supervised by the training process itself. An RL agent can be used to enhance the amount of augmentation when overfitting occurs and can decrease it if the synthetic dataset hinders the validation process, using PPO to update and have actions as both continuous and discrete processes \cite{zhou2024emotion}.

Despite the above, the integration of DRL and generative models for adaptive augmentation, especially within the multimodal EEG and speech emotion recognition domains, is relatively young \cite{busso2008iemocap,latif2020vae}. The major challenge in the above studies is the static nature of the augmentation process and the assumption of homogenous data sources. To address the challenges, we introduce the DRL-controlled GAN-based multimodal EEG and speech emotion recognition model, in which the PPO agent is responsible for adapting the augmentation process, and the late fusion combines the speech emotion recognition signals. The major contributions can be highlighted as follows:
\begin{itemize}
\item A PPO-controlled conditional GAN that adaptively adjusts the EEG augmentation ratio per training epoch, achieving 88.4\% subject-dependent accuracy on the DEAP dataset.
\item A cross-dataset late fusion protocol that integrates EEG (DEAP) and speech (IEMOCAP) modalities without requiring temporal or instance-level alignment.
\item A reward-driven augmentation policy that dynamically balances real and synthetic data based on validation performance, training stability, and sample diversity.
\item Comprehensive evaluation under both subject-dependent and subject-independent protocols, demonstrating a 6.2\% improvement over non-augmented baselines and reduced inter-subject variability.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{EEG-Based Emotion Recognition}
Conventional emotion recognition using EEG techniques relies on manual feature designs, which include power spectral density, differential entropy, and statistical moments, together with shallow learning machines such as support vector machines and random forest machines. Even though these are computationally efficient, they are not capable of handling complex patterns found in EEG signals, and therefore deep learning techniques have been applied to learn the characteristics from the EEGs \cite{chen2024gcn}.

Nevertheless, there exists about 10--20\% performance gap for subject-independent compared to subject-dependent classification. In the first case, there are individual features, which are hard to generalize, and, in the second, there exists interindividual variations, which are more complex but realistic too \cite{hang2019domain}. For the DEAP database, the best results are about 75--85\% for subject-dependent classification and 60--75\% for subject-independent classification \cite{koelstra2012deap}.


\subsection{GAN-Based EEG Augmentation}
GAN-based data augmentation has become popular for handling EEG data limitations. Pan and Zheng developed PSD-GAN for frequency-domain data, proving higher stability, while Bao et al.\ presented VAE-D2GAN for variational and adversarial learning \cite{pan2021gan, bao2021gan}. There are many studies that have reported improvements in accuracy of 3 to 12 percent from GAN-based data augmentation, whereas metrics like FID and IS can also be applied to evaluate the results of generated EEG data \cite{habashi2023gan}.

But most of the current techniques depend upon predefined augmentation ratios and hyperparameters, thus not taking advantage of the training process, which can result in low accuracy and even data drift \cite{luo2020augmentation}. Newer techniques based on diffusions provide better samples but require high computational power to be utilized \cite{siddhad2024diffusion, zhao2024diffusion}.

\subsection{Reinforcement Learning for EEG Analysis}
The method of reinforcement learning was also used for analyzing the EEG signals, which can be seen in the approach called Emotion-Agent by Zhou et al.\ \cite{zhou2024emotion}. Nonetheless, current approaches mostly revolve around hyperparameter optimization and feature selection but not on controlling generative augmentation adaptively \cite{luo2020augmentation}.

One of the key problems associated with reinforcement learning in EEG applications is the credit assignment problem: determining which augmentation action led to a particular validation outcome is challenging because the classifier's performance at any epoch depends on the cumulative effect of all prior augmentation decisions, not just the most recent one. This difficulty is compounded in non-stationary settings where the classifier continuously evolves as it receives new data. Proximal Policy Optimization addresses this by constraining each policy update to a small trust region (controlled by a clipping parameter $\epsilon$), which prevents drastic policy shifts and allows the agent to incrementally attribute performance changes to specific augmentation decisions. Over successive episodes, this conservative update strategy enables the agent to learn a stable mapping from training dynamics to optimal augmentation ratios.
\subsection{Multimodal Emotion Recognition}
Multimodal emotion recognition enhances robustness due to the complementary information from various modalities \cite{wang2022multimodal}. Early fusion usually concatenates features at the input level to learn joint representations and is sensitive to imbalance among the modalities, besides requiring aligned data. IEMOCAP is one of the well-established benchmark datasets for speech-based emotion analysis, including high-quality annotations based on dyadic conversational recordings \cite{busso2008iemocap}.

Late fusion can still be regarded as a viable approach to fuse multimodal information from different datasets due to the simplicity of the approach and ease of interpretation of the results \cite{latif2020vae,abhang2016book,wang2023multimodal}. Through independent training of encoders for specific modalities and their fusion at the decision level, late fusion can bypass the challenges in joint training when performing cross-dataset fusion based on dissimilar annotation structures or data collection protocols. This is particularly exemplary in the fusion of EEG modalities in DEAP and speech in IEMOCAP because they have dissimilar emotion categorization frameworks and design paradigms. The strategy enables flexible missing modality handling during testing and respective preprocessing techniques per modality \cite{latif2020vae}.

\section{Proposed Methodology}

\subsection{Overall Framework}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{framework.png}
\caption{Overall architecture of the proposed adaptive GAN-based EEG--speech emotion recognition framework. DEAP EEG features are augmented via a PPO-controlled GAN, and IEMOCAP speech features are fused via late fusion for classification.}
\label{fig:framework}
\end{figure*}

The suggested framework (see Fig.~\ref{fig:framework}) consists of four modules: EEG features extraction (DEAP), conditional GAN for data augmentation, PPO adaptive control and multimodal late fusion with speech modality (IEMOCAP).

It should be noted that the considered framework works according to the two-stream paradigm. For the EEG stream, a 32-channel DEAP signal is used, where 160-dimensional differential entropy features are extracted and augmented by a conditional GAN. The conditional GAN discriminator evaluates both data plausibility and labels, while PPO is responsible for balancing real and generated data per batch.

For the speech stream, the considered task is solved by extracting 128-dimensional speech features using CNN-LSTM from IEMOCAP dataset.

As illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:framework}, the framework follows a two-stream architecture. The upper stream processes 32-channel DEAP EEG signals: raw signals undergo band-pass filtering and epoching, differential entropy features are extracted across five frequency bands yielding 160-dimensional vectors, and these are fed to both the conditional GAN (for augmentation) and the emotion classifier. The PPO agent sits in a control loop between the GAN output and the classifier, observing validation metrics and adjusting the synthetic-to-real data ratio at each epoch. The lower stream processes IEMOCAP speech recordings through a CNN-LSTM encoder to produce 128-dimensional emotion embeddings. The two streams converge at a late fusion layer, where normalized feature vectors are concatenated and passed to a fully connected classifier for final four-class emotion prediction.

\subsection{EEG Feature Extraction}

For the DEAP data, the EEG signals are preprocessed through a series of steps to improve the signal quality and capture the discriminative features. The signals are band-pass filtered at the range of 4 to 45~Hz to get rid of the noise and drift, and then the signals are cut into epochs of 4 seconds with 50\% overlap for the 32-channel records.

Features of differential entropy (DE) are calculated for five biologically informative frequency bands:
$\delta$ (1--4~Hz), $\theta$ (4--8~Hz), $\alpha$ (8--14~Hz), $\beta$ (14--30~Hz), and $\gamma$ (30--45~Hz). DE features are preferred to the power spectral density because the former offer a more robust and distinguishing EEG signal pattern representation, especially in the context of emotion perception. For every channel and band combination, the DE can be computed as follows:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:de}
h(X) = -\int p(x) \log p(x)\, dx = \frac{1}{2} \log(2\pi e \sigma^2)
\end{equation}
where $X$ follows a Gaussian distribution with variance $\sigma^2$. With a number of 32 channels and 5 frequency bands, this results in a 160-dimensional EEG feature vector per epoch, providing a compact yet informative representation suitable for both GAN-based augmentation and classification.


\subsection{GAN-Based EEG Augmentation}

The conditional GAN model learns the distribution of emotion labeled EEG features, hence allowing class-specific generation of data. This method is more controlled than unconditional GAN since it uses classwise distributions along with the gradient penalty form of Wasserstein loss to prevent mode collapse.

Here, the generator $G$ takes an input random noise vector $z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ and a one-hot encoded class vector $y$.
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:generator}
\hat{x} = G(z, y; \theta_G)
\end{equation}
where $\theta_G$ denotes the generator parameters. The generator network has three fully connected layers that include batch normalization and Leaky ReLU activation functions and performs successive upsampling operations in the latent space to increase the feature space size to 160 dimensions.

The discriminator (critic) network $D$ serves two objectives simultaneously: estimating the Wasserstein distance between real and generated distributions, and predicting the emotion class of each input sample. Intuitively, $D$ acts as both a quality judge---scoring how realistic a sample is---and an emotion classifier that predicts which category the sample belongs to. This dual objective compels the generator to produce samples that are not only statistically similar to real EEG data but also preserve discriminative emotion-related structure:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:discriminator}
D(x, y;\, \theta_D) \;\rightarrow\; (\hat{w},\; \hat{y})
\end{equation}
where $\theta_D$ denotes the discriminator parameters, $x$ is the input feature vector, $y$ is the class conditioning label, $\hat{w} \in \mathbb{R}$ is the Wasserstein critic score (higher values indicate greater realism), and $\hat{y}$ is the predicted emotion class distribution. Unlike standard GANs that output a probability $p_{\text{real}} \in [0, 1]$, the Wasserstein formulation removes the sigmoid activation from the critic output, producing an unbounded score that provides more informative gradients to the generator during training.

Feature-level generation is employed in place of signal generation because of its enhanced statistical stability and robustness against noise. The GAN model is trained with a Wasserstein loss function and gradient penalty to improve training stability. The discriminator is updated five times for every generator update.

To assess the quality of generated EEG features, the Fr\'{e}chet Inception Distance (FID) is adapted to the EEG domain by comparing the mean and covariance statistics of real and synthetic feature distributions in the 160-dimensional DE space. Lower FID values indicate higher fidelity to the real data distribution. Additionally, class-conditional generation quality is monitored through an Inception Score (IS) analog that measures both the confidence and diversity of the classifier's predictions on generated samples. Mode collapse is mitigated through three complementary mechanisms: Wasserstein loss with gradient penalty ($\lambda_{\text{GP}} = 10$), spectral normalization in the discriminator layers, and the 5:1 discriminator-to-generator update ratio, which collectively ensure stable and diverse sample generation throughout training.

\subsection{DRL Agent Design}
Augmentation in the PPO agent (cf. Fig.~\ref{fig:drl_loop}) can be thought of as a sequential decision-making process by means of an episodic MDP formulation.

In each episode $t$, the agent perceives a six-element state vector $s_t$ consisting of training and validation losses, difference in validation loss, prediction variance, synthetic sample diversity, and training progress normalized to one hundred percent. Based on this input state, the PPO agent makes one of five decisions $a_t$, determining how many synthetic samples should be used compared to the number of real samples.

Training the classifier proceeds with the new dataset and the evaluation happens in the validation set. The reward received $r_t$ depends on improvement in the validation set, training stability, and sample diversity. The agent learns from its experience, recording the tuple $(s_t, a_t, r_t, s_{t+1})$ in the replay buffer, and updating the policy once per ten episodes.

This way, the agent is able to use larger proportions of artificial data during early training in order to prevent overfitting and then slowly start to reduce it.

Fig.~\ref{fig:drl_loop} illustrates this control loop: at each epoch, the agent reads six training metrics (state), selects one of five augmentation ratios (action), and the resulting augmented batch trains the classifier. The validation performance then determines the reward signal, closing the feedback loop. This architecture decouples augmentation control from the classifier's gradient flow, ensuring that the PPO agent optimizes the augmentation strategy without interfering with the classifier's learned representations.

\textbf{State Space:} The state captures a snapshot of the current training dynamics, enabling the agent to assess whether the classifier needs more or less synthetic data. The state $s_t$ at epoch $t$ is a 6-dimensional vector:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:state}
s_t = [\mathcal{L}_{\text{val}}^{(t)},\; \mathcal{L}_{\text{train}}^{(t)},\; \Delta\mathcal{L}_{\text{val}},\; \sigma_{\text{pred}},\; \mathcal{H}_{\text{div}},\; t/T]
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}^{(t)}_\text{val}$ and $\mathcal{L}^{(t)}_\text{train}$ are the validation loss and training loss at epoch $t$, respectively, $\Delta \mathcal{L}_\text{val}$ is the difference in validation loss at two consecutive epochs; $\sigma_\text{pred}$ is the standard deviation of the scores associated with prediction uncertainty; $\mathcal{H}_\text{div}$ denotes the diversity of the current synthetic batch, measured as entropy over predicted class probabilities (see Eq.~(\ref{eq:diversity})); and $t / T$ represents normalized training progress.


\textbf{Action Space:} The action space is finite since it involves augmentations intensities $a_t \in \{0,\; 0.25,\; 0.5,\; 0.75,\; 1.0\}$, which represent ratios of synthetic samples to real samples in each training batch. The preference for finite actions compared to continuous ones in the current reinforcement learning task lies in ensuring stable learning process and avoiding pathological values for the ratio of synthetic samples to real samples. The finite action space is developed such that zero actions are possible to avoid the deterioration of the learning process due to synthetic samples.

\textbf{Reward Function:} The reward encourages actions that reduce validation loss while penalizing training instability and promoting diverse generation. It is defined as:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:reward}
r_t = -\Delta \mathcal{L}_{\text{val}} - \lambda |\Delta \mathcal{L}_{\text{train}}| + \mu \mathcal{H}_{\text{div}}
\end{equation}
The coefficient $\lambda = 0.1$ dampens large variations in training losses, which is indicative of unconstrained instability. The coefficient $\mu = 0.05$ provides a small reward for sample diversity. Sample diversity $\mathcal{H}_{\text{div}}$ is measured as entropy in class probability outputs for the generated samples:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:diversity}
\mathcal{H}_{\text{div}} = - \sum_c p(c) \log p(c)
\end{equation}
where $p(c)$ denotes the probability distribution for class $c$ as predicted by the model. The absolute value difference in training losses helps in avoiding any sort of penalty for improvement and rather stabilizes against training instabilities. The most important component is the reward for the validation losses, which drives the agent to optimize for validation set performance rather than training set performance. The constants $\lambda$ and $\mu$ have been settled based on initial results obtained in a validation sweep.

\textbf{PPO Update:} To prevent overly aggressive policy changes that could destabilize the augmentation strategy, the PPO agent uses a clipped surrogate objective:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ppo}
L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_t\!\left[\min\!\left(r_t(\theta)\hat{A}_t,\; \text{clip}(r_t(\theta),\; 1-\epsilon,\; 1+\epsilon)\hat{A}_t\right)\right]
\end{equation}
where $r_t(\theta) = \pi_\theta(a_t|s_t) / \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t|s_t)$ is the ratio between the new and old policy probabilities, and $\hat{A}_t$ denotes the generalized advantage estimate. Clipping threshold $\epsilon = 0.2$ ensures that policy improvement is limited in order to avoid big jumps in its behavior.

The policy function network architecture includes two hidden layers of 64 neurons each with learning rate of $3 \times 10^{-4}$. Even though learning happens along with the classifier, gradients are not backpropagated via classifier’s weights, which makes the system more robust.

On average, PPO stays stable despite the presence of noise coming from interaction with the rest of network components.


\begin{algorithm}
\caption{DRL-Controlled GAN Augmentation}
\label{alg:drl_aug}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE Initialize GAN parameters $\theta_G, \theta_D$
\STATE Initialize classifier parameters $\theta_C$
\STATE Initialize PPO agent with policy $\pi_\theta$
\FOR{each training epoch $t = 1$ to $T$}
\STATE Observe state $s_t$ from training/validation metrics
\STATE Select augmentation action $a_t \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot | s_t)$
\STATE Generate synthetic EEG samples: $\hat{X} = G(z, y)$
\STATE Construct augmented batch: $X_{\text{aug}} = (1-a_t)X + a_t\hat{X}$
\STATE Train classifier on $X_{\text{aug}}$ and update $\theta_C$
\STATE Evaluate on held-out validation set: $\mathcal{L}_{\text{val}}^{(t)}$
\STATE Compute reward $r_t$ using Eq.~(\ref{eq:reward})
\STATE Store transition $(s_t, a_t, r_t, s_{t+1})$ in buffer
\IF{$t \bmod 10 = 0$}
\STATE Update PPO agent using clipped objective and buffer
\ENDIF
\ENDFOR
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Speech Emotion Encoder and Fusion}

\textbf{Speech Emotion Embeddings:}
The speech emotion embeddings are obtained by training a CNN-LSTM model on the IEMOCAP dataset. The CNN layers learn the spectral features of the MFCCs, and the LSTM layers learn the temporal dependencies, resulting in a 128D feature vector.

\textbf{Cross-Dataset Fusion Protocol:}
EEG and speech data are not temporally aligned. Speech is used as emotion prior information at the class level. The label encoding is HVHA-Happiness/Excitement, HVLA-Contentment, LVHA-Anger/Frustration, and LVLA-Sadness. Late fusion is employed after modality-specific models are independently trained.

Both EEG and speech features are normalized (zero mean, unit variance), concatenated, and then input into a fully connected classifier with dropout for predicting emotions.

Late fusion is preferred over early fusion because of the differences between DEAP and IEMOCAP (valence-arousal labeling system versus categorical labeling system and visual-induced paradigm versus conversation scenario). Furthermore, late fusion enables missing modality handling and EEG-only augmentation using GANs without altering speech features.

\section{Experimental Setup}

\subsection{Datasets and Protocols}

The DEAP dataset records the EEG signals of 32 subjects, each performing 40 trials for emotional video clips, which are 1 minute long. The signals are sampled at 512~Hz and recorded through 32 channels, and the classification of emotions is done using valence and arousal scores, which are further categorized into four classes: HVHA, HVLA, LVHA, and LVLA.
The performance of the proposed model is evaluated using two different methods. In case of subject-dependent validation, the model employs 5-fold cross-validation for every subject, whereas subject-independent validation makes use of leave-one-subject-out cross-validation technique, where the model learns from 31 subjects and validates against the other one subject.

The dataset IEMOCAP consists of annotated speech emotion of 10 persons doing dyadic conversations, which include categories such as neutral, happiness, sadness, anger, and frustration. For fusion in the multimodal approach, the classes are aligned to a common label space, and overlapping classes are preserved to have consistent cross-dataset training.
\subsection{Baseline Approaches and Performance Metrics}

Three baseline methods are used for comparison purposes:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{No Data Augmentation:} The classifier is trained using EEG features without any data synthesis process.
\item \textbf{Static Data Augmentation:} The classification network is jointly trained alongside the static GAN with a constant augmentation rate of 50\% during training.
\item \textbf{Unimodal EEG Features:} Proposed framework with data synthesis without speech signal.
\end{itemize}

The model performance is evaluated based on the following three criteria. The accuracy indicates how correct the predictions are, but it produces a biased output when the dataset is imbalanced. Harmonic mean of precision and recall is indicated by F1 score, which is a better measure when the data classes are not balanced. The third criterion is unweighted average recall (UAR). Unweighted Average Recall (UAR) is also provided to account for common class imbalance in EEG emotion datasets. Unweighted Average Recall is a crucial metric to consider while evaluating emotion classification since all emotions are treated equally irrespective of their class frequencies. It avoids cases where the model predicts emotions by simply choosing the dominant class.
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:uar}
\text{UAR} = \frac{1}{C} \sum_{c=1}^{C} \frac{\text{TP}_c}{\text{TP}_c + \text{FN}_c}
\end{equation}
where $C$ is the number of classes, $\text{TP}_c$ denotes true positives for class $c$, and $\text{FN}_c$ denotes false negatives.

\subsection{Implementation Details and Hyperparameters}

The conditional GAN is trained using the Adam optimizer at a learning rate of $2 \times 10^{-4}$ with a batch size of 128 for 200 epochs. Leaky ReLU activation ($\alpha = 0.2$) and batch normalization layers are incorporated into the model. Stability is achieved through the use of Wasserstein loss with gradient penalty of 10. Five discriminator updates are performed for each generator update.

The emotion classifier is designed to be a three-layer dense network (256–128–64), with a dropout ratio of 0.5. This is trained using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of $1 \times 10^{-3}$ and a batch size of 64; cross-entropy loss is employed for training with $1 \times 10^{-4}$ weight decay. Training terminates after 20 epochs of early stopping.

Regarding the PPO agent, the policy network used in the study include two dense layers of with 64 nodes each, $\gamma = 0.99$, $\lambda_{\text{GAE}} = 0.95$, and $\epsilon = 0.2$. Experience is accumulated for 10 epochs before updating the policy using four epochs of mini-batch updates.

\subsection{Analysis of DRL-Guided Augmentation}

The PPO agent, following the procedure in Algorithm~\ref{alg:drl_aug}, implements an adaptive and interpretable augmentation strategy throughout the training epochs. The choice of a high augmentation ratio (0.75--1.0) is made in the early stages of training (epochs 1--30), and then it gradually decreases in the convergence phase (epochs 30--70), and further in the latter stages (epochs 70--100). This contrasts with a static augmentation strategy using a fixed ratio of 50\%, which results in better performance in the subject-independent environment because the proposed method adapts by gradually decreasing the ratio.


\subsection{Multimodal Fusion Benefits}

Multimodal fusion with speech cues obtains additional improvements of 1.3\% in subject-dependent testing and 1.6\% in subject-independent testing, which confirms better generalization capabilities. The information in speech features is more robust across speakers and can be used for mitigating subject-specific variability in EEG data. The qualitative analysis indicates that late fusion especially enhances difficult examples regarding distinguishing neutral and low-arousal emotions.


\subsection{Ablation Studies}
Ablation experiments show the importance of both the role of DRL-based augmentation and the multimodal fusion process. The experiment shows that when we replace DRL-based augmentation by static augmentation of 50\% on our data set, the accuracy decreases to 67.8\%. This indicates the usefulness of adaptive augmentation.

On the other hand, removing the speech signal and keeping only the adaptive DRL-based augmentation produces an accuracy of 70.5\%. Interestingly, using both techniques together improves the performance compared to using each alone.

\subsection{Computational Cost and Limitations}
Compared to conventional augmentation based on the GAN approach, the suggested approach adds around 1.6× to the training time when implemented on one GPU. However, most of the added overhead can be attributed to the frequent PPO training steps, whose impact is still less than that of the classifier training process.

Some weaknesses should be noted as well. First, due to the mismatch between EEG and speech data instances, the latter serves only as a rough indicator of emotions, which might affect performance gains. Second, while comparing the method under consideration with other augmentation approaches, namely those based on diffusions, can provide valuable insights into its effectiveness, no such comparison has been performed. Possible enhancements may involve combining PPO-based augmentation with adaptation techniques to improve generalizability.

\section{Conclusion}
The adaptive GAN-managed EEG-Speech Emotion Recognition system in this paper employs GANs with a deep reinforcement learning model. In this proposed approach, the use of adaptive data augmentation employing Proximal Policy Optimization, along with adaptive multimodal fusion by late fusion, is realized. The proposed idea handles the challenges caused by the scarcity of data and individual variability in the tasks associated with EEG-Speech Emotion Recognition. This proposed idea has been tested on the DEAP and IEMOCAP databases, thus proving the efficacy of reinforcement learning for controlling GANs in Affective Computing. Beyond classification, the recognized emotional states open pathways for predictive applications: continuous mental health monitoring through longitudinal EEG pattern tracking, adaptive human--computer interfaces that respond to user affect in real time, affect-aware recommendation systems that personalize content based on emotional context, and real-time cognitive state assessment for safety-critical environments such as driving or air traffic control. These downstream applications underscore the broader impact of accurate and generalizable emotion recognition frameworks.
